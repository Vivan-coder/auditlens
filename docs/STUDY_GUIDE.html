1. Accounting and audit background

You do not need to be an accountant to understand this project, but you need these ideas to explain it.

1.1 The general ledger and journal entries

A company records every financial event in its general ledger (GL). Each event is a journal entry, made of two or more lines. Each line debits or credits an account from the chart of accounts (a numbered list such as 1000 Cash, 2000 Accounts Payable, 6300 Professional Fees).

The one rule that makes the whole system checkable: in every entry, total debits equal total credits. That is why the generator and the CSV validator both test that entries balance.

Worked example. The company receives a consultant's invoice for 1,500 BHD:

Line Account Debit Credit
1 6300 Professional Fees (an expense) 1,500.000
2 2000 Accounts Payable (what we owe) 1,500.000

Thirty days later the company pays it:

Line Account Debit Credit
1 2000 Accounts Payable (debt cleared) 1,500.000
2 1000 Cash at Bank (cash goes out) 1,500.000

This is exactly how invoice and payment entries are built in synth/context.py (make_invoice, make_payment). The code recognises them later purely from the accounts: an invoice credits Accounts Payable (2000); a payment debits 2000 and credits Cash (1000). The account numbers are in config.yaml under accounts: so another company's chart of accounts can be used.

In this project one journal line is a row in journal_entries.csv, and one entry is all rows sharing an entry_id. About 49,464 lines make 24,714 entries; most entries have two lines, and the 12 monthly payroll entries have five.

1.2 Fields that matter for audit

Field Meaning Why an auditor looks at it
document_date Date on the source document (invoice date) Compare with posting date to spot backdating
posting_date When it was recorded in the ledger Weekends, holidays, late postings
period Accounting month the entry belongs to (2025-03) Entries into closed periods

vendor_id, invoice_number Supplier and its document number Duplicates, sequences, ghost vendors

Approval limits. Staff can only approve up to a limit tied to their role. In this project: accountant 25,000; senior accountant 100,000; financial controller 500,000; CFO 5,000,000; AP clerks cannot approve. The generator always picks the lowest-limit user who outranks the creator and can cover the amount (GenContext.approver_for), so clean data never breaks the hierarchy.

Period close. Each month is "closed" 5 days after month end (calendar.period_close_days); after that, postings to that month are suspicious. March 2025 closes on 5 April 2025.

Bahrain context. The weekend is Friday and Saturday, not Saturday and Sunday. Public holidays come from the holidays library's Bahrain calendar. The currency is the Bahraini dinar (BHD), which has 3 decimal places (fils), so amounts look like 1,500.000. This is handled in bh_calendar.py, and it is a detail that shows you thought about the local context.

1.3 What auditors do with journal entries

Auditing standards (ISA 240, on the auditor's responsibilities relating to fraud) require auditors to test journal entries and other adjustments, because management can override controls by posting unusual entries. The traditional approach is to pick a sample by hand. With tens of thousands of entries, that is slow and misses things. Journal entry analytics means testing the whole population with computer tests that mirror what an auditor would look for, then reviewing the exceptions.

Typical risk indicators (all implemented here): duplicate payments, payments or purchases arranged to sit just below an approval limit, entries on weekends and holidays, round amounts, the same person creating and approving, entries into closed periods or backdated, vendors that share details with employees, vendor bank changes before large payments, gaps in invoice numbers, unusual account combinations, amounts that violate Benford's Law, and large manual entries near period end.

1.4 The key idea to keep repeating

A flag is a lead, not a finding. The system narrows 24,714 entries to a ranked list with reasons; an auditor still gathers evidence and uses judgement.

This phrase appears in the app, the Excel cover sheet and the README on purpose. Using it shows you understand the limits of analytics in an audit.

2.3 The two-minute explanation (memorise the shape, not the words)

"AuditLens is a journal-entry analytics tool. Auditors have to look for fraud and error in tens of thousands of ledger entries, which is impossible by hand, so I built something that tests the whole ledger. There are 23 rule-based audit tests in eight families, such as duplicate payments, split purchases just under approval limits, weekend and holiday postings, and vendors that share a bank account with an employee. On top of that an unsupervised Isolation Forest scores how statistically unusual each entry is. I combine them into a risk score and a ranked review queue, and every flag comes with a plain-English reason. Because I had no real client data, I generated a realistic synthetic ledger for a fictional Bahraini company and planted twelve kinds of known anomalies, kept in a separate label file, so I could measure precision and recall honestly. The rules caught everything they were designed for, but I also built an adversarial challenge set and I am upfront about the limits: threshold-based rules miss cases just outside their thresholds, and the ML model alone is only about 13% precise. It is a screening tool that gives an auditor a ranked list of leads with reasons."

Then pause. They will ask a follow-up; the rest of this guide prepares you for those.

3. The data: how the synthetic ledger is built and why

3.1 Why synthetic data at all?

Real general ledgers are confidential and I have no access to client data. More importantly, real data has no answer key: you never know which entries are truly fraudulent, so you cannot measure whether a detector works. A synthetic ledger lets me plant anomalies I know about and then check how many the system finds. The price is that the result only proves the system works on data like mine (see Part 8 for how honestly that is handled).

Everything is reproducible: generation.seed: 42 in config.yaml feeds one NumPy random generator and one Faker generator, and no other randomness is used. The same seed always produces byte-identical data (a test checks this).

3.2 How the generator is organised

generate.py is a thin orchestrator. The real work is in synth/:

File Responsibility
master_data.py Chart of accounts, users and roles, 110 regular vendors in 7 categories, 8 recurring vendors, 60 employees, and a few legitimate vendor bank changes
context.py GenContext: shared state (random generator, entries, labels) and helpers such as approver_for, sample_dates, resolve_dates, make_invoice, make_payment, make_journal
baseline.py All the clean, normal activity
inject_amounts.py, inject_timing.py, inject_vendors.py, inject_accounts.py One injector per anomaly type

Order of work: build the clean baseline first, then plant anomalies on top, then number the entries and write files. Labels are collected in a list while planting and written to a separate labels.csv.

3.3 The clean baseline (what "normal" looks like)

Activity Entries (approx.) Accounting Notes
Sales invoices 5,000 Dr 1100 Receivables / Cr 4000 or 4100 Revenue Automated; sequential numbers SI-000001...
Customer receipts up to 94% of sales (those due before year end) Dr 1000 Cash / Cr 1100 Paid 5-120 days later (average 35); 70% automated
Vendor invoices ~7,700 + 96 recurring Dr expense (by vendor category) / Cr 2000 70% manual (keyed by the vendor's AP clerk), 30% automated
Vendor payments one per invoice due before year end Dr 2000 / Cr 1000 Manual; paid at the vendor's terms (30/45/60 days) with a few days' jitter
Payroll 12 Dr 6000 (one line per department) / Cr 1000 Automated, last day of the month
Depreciation 12 Dr 6900 / Cr 1590 Automated, 18,250.500 each month
Accruals and reversals ~380 accruals + reversals on the next month's first business day Dr expense / Cr 2100, then the reverse Manual
Reclassifications ~140 Dr one expense / Cr another, from three fixed pairs Manual

Realism features, and why each exists

  • Month-end spike. sample_dates gives the last three business days of each month a weight of 2.6 and the first business day 1.4, so volume surges at close, as in real ledgers.
  • Seasonality. Monthly weights in config.yaml (sales_seasonality, purchase_seasonality): a dip in March (Ramadan), a strong Q4.
  • Recurring vendors. Rent (a constant 4,500.000), electricity, telecom, cleaning, security, software and insurance (quarterly) bill on a schedule. This matters because a contract amount repeating is normal, and the round-amount test must not flag it.
  • Business-day logic. Documents are dated on business days (Sunday to Thursday, not holidays). Manual entries are posted 0-3 days later (probabilities 55%, 25%, 12%, 8%) and rolled to the next business day. Automated month-end entries (payroll, depreciation) can fall on a weekend, which is realistic and is why the weekend test applies only to manual entries.
  • Posting period rule (GenContext.resolve_dates). An entry belongs to its document month if it is posted within the close window; otherwise to the posting month. So a 31 March invoice posted on 2 April still belongs to March (inside the 5-day close window), and clean data never trips the post-close test.
  • Vendor numbering. Each vendor's invoices get increasing numbers (INV-000123) rising by 5-15 each time, as the vendor has other customers. invoice_number_for finds the right number for a date. If injected invoices use up a gap, shift_invoice_numbers moves later numbers up (see the bug story in Part 11).

3.4 Why amounts follow Benford's Law (the one clever trick)

Benford's Law says the leading digit d of many real-world amounts appears with probability log10(1 + 1/d). To generate normal amounts that follow it exactly, the generator uses:

amount = 10^(d + u), where d is a whole number (an order of magnitude) and u is uniform between 0 and 1.

The first digit of an amount depends only on the fractional part of its log10, which is u. If u is uniform, the first digit is exactly Benford-distributed, whatever mix of orders of magnitude d you choose. So each vendor category has its own mix (goods suppliers 100-100,000; office supplies 10-10,000 and so on) but all conform. Test: the clean invoices pass a chi-square Benford test (p about 0.11). The 3-decimal rounding does not disturb it.

3.5 Master data

  • Users come from config.yaml roles: 3 AP clerks, 3 accountants, 2 senior accountants, 1 financial controller, 1 CFO, plus a SYSTEM user for automated postings.
  • AP clerk portfolios. Each vendor category belongs to one clerk (clerk 0: goods and office supplies; clerk 1: professional services, marketing, IT; clerk 2: travel, repairs and the recurring vendors). So a clerk's pattern of accounts is stable, which is what makes "rare user-account" detection meaningful.
  • Vendors and employees have Bahrain-style addresses and IBAN-shaped bank accounts (BH + 2 digits + 4 bank letters + 14 digits; the check digits are not real).
  • 10% of vendors are onboarded during the year; they never invoice before they exist.
  • Three vendors have legitimate bank-account changes, so a bank change by itself is not suspicious.

labels.csv has entry_id, anomaly_type, role (injected, or counterpart for the genuine original of a duplicated payment), related_entry_id and a human note. 3.7 Check your understanding

  1. Why do planted anomalies need valid approvers and normal posting lags? (So each is caught by its own test, and a test passing because of a side-effect would not fool us.)
  2. Why do automated entries have no approver? (They are system postings; the approval tests target manual ones.)
  3. Why does the generator create duplicate payments with a different invoice number a third of the time? (So a test that only matches invoice numbers cannot catch them all; the amount-based test must.)

4. The audit tests (rules): one by one

4.1 How every rule is built (the common pattern)

Each module in rules/ exposes run(Dataset) and returns a flags table with five columns: entry_id, family, rule_code, reason, related_entry_id. The helper make_flags in rules/base.py builds it, and combine merges tables and removes duplicate (entry, code) pairs. run_all_rules in rules/__init__.py calls every family.

Two design rules to be able to state:

  • Thresholds are never hardcoded. Every number below is read from config.yaml under rules: (pydantic validates it in config.py). Change the file, not the code.
  • Every flag explains itself. The reason is a full sentence with the specific numbers, e.g. "Posted 2025-05-06 into period 2025-03, which closed 2025-04-05".

Rules work on the entry-level table built by dataset.build_entries (one row per entry with amount = total debit, sets of debit and credit accounts, and is_invoice / is_payment identified from the accounts). Rules that need master data (vendors, employees, users, bank changes) silently skip when it is missing, so an uploaded journal-only CSV still gets the other tests.

4.2 Duplicate payments (rules/duplicates.py)

Story. Paying the same invoice twice is among the most common and expensive accounts-payable errors, and an easy way to siphon cash.

Code Fires when Weight
DUP_SAME_INVOICE Two payments to one vendor reference the same invoice number (a trailing re-key suffix such as -R is stripped) 40
DUP_EXACT_AMOUNT Same vendor, identical amount, within 7 days (window_days) 35
DUP_NEAR_AMOUNT Same vendor, amounts within 0.5% (amount_tolerance_pct), within 7 days 8

How it works. Take all payments (ds.payments), self-join them on vendor_id (and on the normalised invoice number for the first test), keep each pair once (entry_id < entry_id_o), apply the date and amount conditions, and flag both entries of the pair, each pointing at the other via related_entry_id. Normalising the invoice number is re.sub(r"\D+$", "", s): remove trailing non-digits.

Real example. "Payment of 34,542.874 to vendor V0025 on 2025-03-02 is identical to 34,542.874 paid to the same vendor on 2025-03-03 (JE002532)."

Why three codes? Evidence strength differs. An identical amount to the fils is very unlikely by chance; "near" is not (large vendors send similar invoices), so it gets a low weight. I split them after a first run showed 82 near-amount flags with only 20% real hits.

What it misses. A repeat after more than 7 days, or an amount more than 0.5% different with a new invoice number, passes. The challenge set measures exactly this (Part 8).

4.3 Approval and segregation of duties (rules/approval.py)

Story. The approval hierarchy is the main preventive control over spending. Defeating it, or approving your own work, is a classic fraud and error pathway.

Code Fires when Weight
SPLIT_BELOW_LIMIT At least 3 (min_count) invoices from one creator to one vendor, each 90%-100% of an approval limit (band_low_pct), within 10 days (window_days) 35
SELF_APPROVED A manual entry's creator equals its approver 35
NO_APPROVER A manual entry has no approver 30
APPROVER_OVER_LIMIT Entry amount exceeds the approver's limit (from the users table) 25

Split purchases in detail. Limits come from the roles in config.yaml (25,000; 100,000; 500,000; 5,000,000). For each invoice, _near_limit tests whether 0.90 x limit <= amount < limit. Candidates are grouped by (vendor, creator) and sorted by date. A sliding window with numpy.searchsorted finds, for each candidate, how many fall within 10 days; if at least 3, all of them are flagged. Total value is quoted in the reason: "97,757.382 is just under the 100,000 approval limit; 3 such invoices from fdawood to vendor V0011 within 10 days total 294,420.824." (A 294k purchase that would have needed CFO approval was approved at lower levels.)

What it misses. Splits at 80-88% of the limit, only two parts, or spread across several people (all tested in the challenge set). Collusion (A approves B's entries and B approves A's) does not equal self-approval.

4.4 Timing (rules/timing.py)

Story. Few people have a legitimate reason to key manual entries when the office is closed. Entries slipped into closed periods or dated far back are a way to move results between periods.

Code Fires when Weight
WEEKEND_POSTING Manual entry posted on Friday or Saturday 25
HOLIDAY_POSTING Manual entry posted on a weekday Bahrain public holiday 25
POST_CLOSE_POSTING Posting date is after the period's close date (month end + 5 days) 35
BACKDATED_POSTING Posting date is more than 30 days after the document date 25

How. Weekend uses day_of_week in (4, 5) (Monday = 0, so Friday = 4). Holidays come from bh_calendar.bahrain_holidays. The close date is PeriodIndex(period).to_timestamp(how="end") + 5 days. Only entries with source in rules.timing.weekend_sources (manual) are tested for weekend and holiday, because payroll and depreciation legitimately run on the last calendar day.

Cross-talk to know. A post-close entry usually also has a long posting lag, so it often gets both POST_CLOSE_POSTING and BACKDATED_POSTING. Noisy-OR (Part 7) handles that sensibly.

4.5 Amounts (rules/amounts.py)

Story. Real transactions produce messy amounts; round ones suggest estimates or invention. Large manual journals near period end are a recognised management-override and earnings-management risk.

Code Fires when Weight
ROUND_AMOUNT Manual entry of at least 5,000 that is an exact multiple of 1,000, and not a repeating contract amount 25
LARGE_MANUAL_PERIOD_END Manual entry at or above 250,000 (materiality), posted in the last 3 business days of the month 40
LARGE_MANUAL Same size, but not near period end 25

The modulo trick. Testing amount % 1000 == 0 with floats is fragile (1000.0000000001). The code uses ((amount + 500) % 1000) - 500 and checks it is close to 0, which maps every remainder into the range -500 to +500 so values just below or above a multiple both land near 0.

Contract exemption. If the same (vendor, amount) pair repeats at least 6 times (recurring_repeat_exempt), it is treated as a contract (rent), not a red flag. The challenge set shows the limit: a contract paid only four times is flagged.

4.6 Vendor master data (rules/vendors.py)

Story. A vendor that shares an employee's bank account is the textbook fictitious-vendor scheme. A changed bank account followed quickly by a large payment is the textbook business-email-compromise pattern.

Code Fires when Weight
GHOST_VENDOR_BANK A vendor's bank account equals an employee's 45
GHOST_VENDOR_ADDRESS A vendor's address equals an employee's 35
BANK_CHANGE_LARGE_PAYMENT A payment of at least 20,000 and at least 5x the vendor's median payment, 0-14 days after a bank change 35

How. Text is normalised (lowercase, keep only letters and digits) and merged on equality, so bh11 AAAA... and BH11AAAA... match. Every entry posted to a flagged vendor is flagged, because the risk attaches to the vendor. For bank changes, payments are merged with the change history on vendor, filtered to the window, and compared with the vendor's median payment. Reason: "Payment of 90,448.425 to vendor V0071 1 days after its bank account changed on 2025-02-10 (vendor median payment 2,423.648)."

What it misses. Reformatted addresses ("Bldg" vs "Building") and payments more than 14 days after the change. A fuzzy-matching upgrade is on the "next steps" list.

4.7 Invoice-number sequences (rules/sequence.py)

Story. Vendor invoices normally arrive in numeric order. A jump may mean documents were removed or numbers invented; a lower number arriving later suggests backdating or fabrication; a repeated number suggests a duplicate.

Code Fires when Weight
INVOICE_GAP Number jumps by more than 50 (max_gap) from the previous 25
INVOICE_OUT_OF_ORDER Number is lower than one already received 30
DUPLICATE_INVOICE_NUMBER The same vendor invoice number booked twice 30

The cummax trick. For each vendor, invoices are sorted by document date, then number. prev_max is the running maximum of earlier numbers (cummax().shift()), and step = number - prev_max. If step > 50 it is a gap; if step < 0 it is out of order. Using the running maximum (not just the previous row) means one late low number flags only itself, not the invoices after it.

4.8 Account usage (rules/pairings.py)

Story. A journal that moves value between accounts never otherwise linked (e.g. Suspense paid from Bank), or a user posting to an account outside their normal duties, are classic signs of error or override.

Code Fires when Weight
RARE_ACCOUNT_PAIR The (debit account, credit account) pair appears in at most 3 entries all year 30
RARE_USER_ACCOUNT A user with at least 50 manual entries posts to an account in at most 3 of them 25

How. Count how many entries use each pair (or each user-account combination) and flag the rare ones. Rarity is judged against the ledger's own history, not a fixed list, so it adapts to any company. The 50-entry minimum stops a new user's few entries being "rare" by definition.

Real example. The top entry in the queue, JE007482: "Debit 6000 / credit 2000 is an unusual account pairing: seen in only 3 entries all year" and "jkooheji posted to account 6000 in only 1 of their 142 manual entries."

4.9 Summary of weights

Weights express how specific and trustworthy a test is (0-100). They are judgement calls from audit practice, not fitted to the labels. The highest are the most specific (ghost vendor bank 45; large period-end journal 40; duplicate by invoice 40); the lowest are noisy (near-duplicate amount 8; Benford at vendor level 15).

5. Benford's Law in depth

This is the most mathematical test, and the one interviewers like to probe. Make sure you can explain it from scratch.

5.1 The idea

In many real collections of amounts (invoices, populations, river lengths), the first digit is not equally likely to be 1 through 9. The digit 1 leads about 30% of the time and 9 only about 5%. The exact probability that the first digit is d is:

P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d)

Digit 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9
Expected share 30.1% 17.6% 12.5% 9.7% 7.9% 6.7% 5.8% 5.1% 4.6%

Intuition. Quantities that grow multiplicatively spend a long time starting with 1. To go from 1,000 to 2,000 a quantity must double (100% growth); to go from 8,000 to 9,000 it grows only 12.5%. On a logarithmic scale (which is how multiplicative growth looks), the stretch from 1,000 to 2,000 is much longer than from 9,000 to 10,000, so more values fall there. The formula is just the length of each digit's stretch on a log scale.

Why it helps auditors. People who invent numbers tend to spread digits roughly evenly, or favour certain digits, or cluster just below thresholds (e.g. invoices of 9,900 to stay under 10,000). Those habits break the pattern.

5.2 When it does NOT apply (say this unprompted in an interview)

  • Numbers assigned rather than measured (phone numbers, invoice numbers).
  • Amounts bounded by contract or price list, or limited to a narrow range (a vendor that always bills 800-1,200).
  • Too few observations.
  • Data that does not span several orders of magnitude.

That is why a Benford flag is a lead, not proof, and why AuditLens tests per vendor only when there are at least 50 invoices (rules.benford.min_invoices).

5.3 The test: chi-square goodness of fit

For a vendor with n invoices, count how many start with each digit (observed, O). Under Benford the expected count for digit d is E = n x P(d). The statistic is:

chi-square = sum over d=1..9 of (O_d - E_d)^2 / E_d

Big values mean the observed counts are far from expected. With 9 digit categories there are 8 degrees of freedom (the counts must add up to n, which removes one). From the chi-square distribution we get a p-value: the probability of a deviation at least this large if the vendor really followed Benford. A tiny p-value means "this would almost never happen by chance."

The threshold. We flag when p < 0.001 (rules.benford.p_threshold). For 8 degrees of freedom that corresponds to a chi-square above about 26.1. It is deliberately strict because we test many vendors (see 5.5).

5.4 Worked example: the planted vendor V0122 (real numbers)

V0122 has 60 invoices whose amounts were drawn uniformly between 7,000 and 9,990, so every amount starts with 7, 8 or 9.

Digit Benford share Expected count (E, of 60) Observed (O) (O-E)^2 / E
1 30.1% 18.06 0 18.1
2 17.6% 10.57 0 10.6
3 12.5% 7.50 0 7.5
4 9.7% 5.81 0 5.8
5 7.9% 4.75 0 4.8
6 6.7% 4.02 0 4.0
7 5.8% 3.48 17 52.5
8 5.1% 3.07 21 104.8
9 4.6% 2.75 22 135.0
Total 60 343.0

chi-square = 343.0, far above 26.1, so p = 2.8 x 10^-69: essentially impossible by chance. The mean absolute deviation (MAD, the average gap between observed and expected shares) is 0.188. The dashboard's Benford page shows this chart: blue bars only at 7, 8, 9 against a dotted Benford curve.

5.5 Multiple testing (the subtle point)

We run the test on 55 vendors (those with at least 50 invoices). If every vendor were genuinely Benford and we used p < 0.05, we would expect about 2.75 false alarms (55 x 0.05). With p < 0.001 we expect about 0.055, i.e. usually none. In our data exactly one vendor fails: the planted one. This is the reasoning behind the strict threshold. (A related point: chi-square is an approximation that is best when every expected count is at least 5; at n = 50 the expected count for digit 9 is 2.3, so the p-value is approximate, which is another reason to be strict and to call the result a lead.)

5.6 In the code

rules/benford.py:

  • first_digits(amounts): for each positive amount, exponent = floor(log10(a)) and digit = a // 10**exponent.
  • digit_counts: numpy.bincount into digits 1-9.
  • benford_table(ds): for each vendor with enough invoices, runs scipy.stats.chisquare(observed, expected) and records n, chi-square, p-value, MAD and the observed share of each digit. It feeds both the rule and the dashboard.
  • run(ds): for every vendor with p below the threshold, flags all of that vendor's invoices as BENFORD_VENDOR. The test population is invoices only (credits to payables), not payments, because payments repeat the same amounts and would double-count.

Weight 15, and why. It implicates a whole vendor, not one entry, so the weight is low; flagging 60 invoices at a high weight would flood the queue with one lead. In the combined view it sits below the alert threshold of 20 by design (the Benford page is where you read it).

Challenge result. A fabricated vendor with only 40 invoices is missed because it is below the 50-invoice minimum.

6. The machine learning layer

6.1 Why add ML to a rules-based tool?

Rules only find what someone thought to write a rule for. An unsupervised model, which needs no labelled fraud, flags entries that are statistically odd compared with the rest, including patterns nobody anticipated. It is the right type of ML here because in real life you rarely have trustworthy fraud labels (you only know about frauds that were caught).

6.2 Isolation Forest: the intuition

Imagine plotting entries as points. A normal entry sits inside a dense crowd; to isolate it from the others you need many cuts. An unusual entry sits alone; a few random cuts separate it.

The algorithm:

  1. Build many random binary trees. At each node pick a random feature and a random split value between that feature's min and max (for the sample at that node). Keep splitting until each point is alone.
  2. For a given entry, measure its path length (number of splits to isolate it) in each tree and average.
  3. Short average path = easy to isolate = anomalous. Long path = blends in = normal.

The formal score (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008) is s = 2^(-E[h(x)] / c(n)), where E[h(x)] is the average path length and c(n) = 2(ln(n-1) + 0.5772) - 2(n-1)/n is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary tree, used to normalise. For a subsample of 256, c = 10.2. An entry isolated in 4 splits scores about 0.76 (quite anomalous); one needing 10.2 splits scores 0.5 (ordinary).

Settings used (ml: in config.yaml, ml/model.py):

  • n_estimators: 300 trees. More trees make the score more stable.
  • Each tree is built on a random subsample of 256 entries (scikit-learn's default max_samples="auto" is min(256, n)), which keeps it fast and works well for rare anomalies.
  • random_state = 42, so results are reproducible.
  • contamination="auto". We do not use the model's own yes/no prediction; we use the continuous score.

scikit-learn's score_samples returns the negative of s, so the code uses -score_samples(...) to make higher = more anomalous.

From score to percentile. ml_score = rank(score, pct=True) turns the raw score into a percentile between 0 and 1 (1.0 = the most anomalous entry). A percentile is easy to threshold ("top 2%") and to combine with rule evidence, and it does not depend on the raw scale of the model.

ML flag. Entries with ml_score >= 0.98 (ml.flag_percentile) get an ML_ANOMALY flag, about 2% of entries (495 of 24,714).

6.3 The 20 features (ml/features.py)

The model sees one row of numbers per entry. Trees are insensitive to monotone scaling, so no normalisation is needed. All features are computed from the journal (and the users table when present); no labels, and none of the generator's internal fields, are used.

Group Features Why they matter
Size log_amount, n_lines, is_round Unusually large, many-line or round entries
Account norms account_amount_z Largest
Vendor behaviour has_vendor, vendor_count_log, vendor_amount_z, amount_to_vendor_median Is this amount typical for this vendor? How established is the vendor?
Twin entries twin_reldiff, twin_gap_days How close is the amount (relative difference) and date to the neighbouring entry of the same vendor and type? A duplicate-payment signature. Defaults: 1.0 and 365 when there is no neighbour

Deliberately excluded. The rule signals themselves (self-approved, near-limit, missing approver) are not features. If they were, the model would just rediscover the rules and the "ML versus rules" comparison would be meaningless. The ML layer is meant to be an independent second opinion.

Z-score reminder. z = (x - mean) / standard deviation: how many standard deviations an amount is from the average. Amounts span orders of magnitude, so z-scores are computed on the log of the amount.

6.4 Explaining an ML flag in plain language

A raw anomaly score is useless to an auditor ("why?"). So explain() names the three features that deviate most from similar entries:

  1. Peers are defined by (is_manual, has_vendor): four groups (manual or automated, vendor or not). Comparing a manual vendor invoice with automated payroll would make every manual entry look odd.
  2. Within the group, each feature gets a robust z-score: (x - median) / (1.4826 x MAD), where MAD is the median absolute deviation. Medians and MADs are not distorted by the very outliers we are hunting. The constant 1.4826 makes MAD comparable to a standard deviation for normal data. If MAD is 0 the code falls back to the standard deviation, then to 1.
  3. Take the three features with the largest |z| and print value and the peer median.

Real reason text: "Statistically unusual entry (anomaly score in the top 2%): rarity of this user/account combination: 2.15 (typical for similar entries 0.18); amount as a multiple of the approver's limit: 0.17 (typical for similar entries 0.03); ..."

6.5 How good is it? (the honest answer)

  • ML alone: 495 flagged, 66 genuinely planted, so precision 13.3%, recall 16.0% (about 8 times better than random at 1.7% prevalence, but still weak).
  • It finds entries that are numerically odd: 50% of the large period-end journals, 49% of unusual account use, 31% of split purchases.
  • It finds 0% of duplicate payments, Benford vendors, sequence breaks and weekend postings, and 2% of ghost vendors. Why: a duplicated payment looks like any other payment on its own; a ghost vendor's invoices look like any invoice. Those anomalies are about relationships (this payment versus that one), not about one entry being numerically extreme, so a model that looks at one entry at a time cannot see them.
  • On the challenge set, it did catch all six journals sitting just under the materiality threshold that the fixed rule missed, which is exactly the complementary value ML is meant to add.

So in AuditLens ML is a second opinion that surfaces odd entries no rule describes, not a detector to trust on its own. Saying this plainly is a strength, not a weakness.

7. The risk score and the review queue

7.1 Why combine at all?

Auditors have limited time. They need one ranked list, not 23 separate rule outputs plus a model score. The score answers "which entries first?".

7.2 Rule evidence: the noisy-OR

Each rule has a weight w between 0 and 100 (scoring.rule_weights), meaning roughly "how much does this signal alone raise suspicion". When several rules fire on one entry, we combine them with the noisy-OR:

rule_score = 100 x ( 1 - product over fired rules of (1 - w/100) )

Think of each rule as an independent detector that "catches" a true problem with probability w/100. The entry is suspicious if at least one detector would catch it, so we compute the chance that none does (product of (1 - w/100)) and subtract from 1.

Worked example (the top entry, JE007482). Two rules fired: RARE_ACCOUNT_PAIR (w = 30) and RARE_USER_ACCOUNT (w = 25).

  • chance neither catches it = 0.70 x 0.75 = 0.525
  • rule_score = 100 x (1 - 0.525) = 47.5

Compare the alternatives: a plain sum would give 55 and could exceed 100 with enough rules; the maximum would give only 30 and ignore the second rule. Noisy-OR sits in between: more evidence always raises the score, with diminishing returns, and it can never exceed 100. It is also order-independent and easy to explain.

7.4 ML points

ML only adds points to entries that are already ML-flagged (top 2%):

ml_points = 30 x (ml_score - 0.98) / (1 - 0.98), clipped at 0

So the most anomalous entry gets the full 30 points, an entry at the 99th percentile gets 15, and everything below the 98th gets 0. For JE007482 the ML score is essentially 1.0, so about 29.6 points.

risk_score = min(100, rule_score + ml_points) = 47.5 + 29.6 = about 77

Why only for flagged entries? Every entry that scores ML points also carries an ML_ANOMALY flag and therefore a written reason. Early in development entries between the 95th and 98th percentile got points but no reason; a test caught it and the design was changed so that one threshold controls both. Why cap at 30? So that rules (specific and explainable) carry most of the weight, and the model cannot push an entry with no rule evidence above the Medium tier by itself.

7.5 Tiers and the queue

  • Tiers (scoring.risk_tiers): High at 50 or more, Medium from 20 to 50, Low below 20. Two rules fire, or one strong rule plus a high ML score, to reach High. A single ML flag at maximum (30 points) is Medium.
  • Queue (ml/scoring.py: build_review_queue): all entries with risk_score >= 1, sorted by risk score, then ML score, then amount. 903 entries: 71 High, 427 Medium, 405 Low. Columns: rank, risk score and tier, rule score, ML score, number of flags, rule codes, concatenated reasons, related entry ids, and the entry's details.

7.6 The executive summary (summary.py)

  • split_attention divides the High and Medium entries into rule-backed (rule_score above 0: 360 entries) and ML-only (138). ML-only is shown last and labelled lower-confidence because ML alone is about 13% precise.
  • themes groups rule-backed High/Medium entries by rule family, with an auditor-style suggested follow-up procedure for each (a dictionary THEMES). ML-only entries form their own last theme.
  • headline writes the paragraph at the top of the Overview.

All of it is derived from flags and scores only; a test checks it never touches the labels.

8. Evaluation: how we know whether it works

8.1 The setup

Because the generator wrote a separate answer key (labels.csv), we can score the system like an exam. The unit is one journal entry. A positive is any entry that appears in the label file (this includes the genuine original of a duplicated payment, because the test is supposed to flag both). There are 412 positives among 24,714 entries, so 24,302 are clean.

For one system (say, "rules alone") each entry falls in one of four boxes:

Truly anomalous Truly clean
Flagged True positive (TP) False positive (FP)
Not flagged False negative (FN) True negative (TN)

8.2 The metrics, with the real numbers

  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): "of what we flagged, how much was real?" It is the auditor's wasted-time measure.
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN): "of what was real, how much did we find?" It is the missed-risk measure.
  • F1 = 2 x precision x recall / (precision + recall): one number balancing both (the harmonic mean, which punishes a system that is good at only one).
  • False-positive rate (FPR) = FP / (clean entries): the share of clean entries wrongly flagged.
System Flagged TP FP FN Precision Recall F1
Rules alone (any rule fired) 490 412 78 0 84.1% 100% 0.914
ML alone (top 2% of anomaly score) 495 66 429 346 13.3% 16.0% 0.146
Combined (risk score 20 or more) 498 348 150 64 69.9% 84.5% 0.765

Reading: the rules found everything (they were built for these anomalies) at the cost of 78 false alarms. ML alone is mostly noise. The combined view misses 64: 60 are the Benford vendor's invoices (weight 15, below the alert threshold of 20 by design) and 4 are weak near-amount duplicates.

Confidence intervals. A proportion measured on a finite sample is uncertain. The report gives 95% Wilson score intervals, which behave well for small samples and for values near 0 or 100%. With p = k/n and z = 1.96:

centre = (p + z^2/2n) / (1 + z^2/n); half-width = z x sqrt(p(1-p)/n + z^2/4n^2) / (1 + z^2/n)

For the rules' precision (412/490) this gives 81% to 87%.

8.3 Per-type metrics and how they are defined

The project specification asks for precision, recall and F1 per anomaly type. The definitions (stated in the report too):

  • Recall for a type = share of that type's planted entries that the system flagged at all.
  • Precision for a type (rules and combined) = of the flagged entries that carry a rule designed for that type, the share that really are that type. The mapping from type to rule codes is TYPE_CODES in evaluate.py.
  • Precision for ML = its yield: the share of all ML flags that are that type (ML has no designed type).

Recall by type for the rules is 100% everywhere; for ML it ranges from 0% to 50%; for the combined score it is 100% except duplicates (92%, the 4 near-amount-only cases) and Benford (0%).

8.4 Precision at K (the auditor-capacity view)

If reviewers can only look at K entries, what matters is how clean the top K of the ranked list is. Precision@K = (real anomalies among the top K) / K.

System Precision@50 Precision@100
Rules alone (ranked by rule score, ties by amount) 100% 100%
ML alone (ranked by anomaly score) 28% 21%
Combined (ranked by risk score) 100% 100%

So the top 100 of the queue are all real. Recall@100 is only 24%, simply because there are 412 anomalies and K = 100 (the maximum possible is 100/412).

8.5 The base-rate problem (the most important caveat)

In the synthetic data, 1.7% of entries are anomalous. In a real ledger the rate of true problems is far lower (assume 0.1%). Precision depends on how common positives are. Use the rules' own numbers: recall about 100% and FPR = 78 / 24,302 = 0.32%. Per 100,000 real entries:

  • 100 true problems -> about 100 caught,
  • 99,900 clean entries x 0.32% = about 321 false alarms.

precision = 100 / (100 + 321) = about 24%

That is the formula precision = R x pi / (R x pi + FPR x (1 - pi)) with pi = 0.001. The same system that looks 84% precise here would hand an auditor roughly three false alarms per real problem. This is the single most honest thing in the project; say it before anyone asks.

8.6 Stability across seeds and the threshold choice

  • Seeds. Regenerating with four other seeds (1001-1004) and re-running everything gave rules 84.9% +/- 4.0% precision and 100% recall; ML 13.9% +/- 2.4% and 16.7% +/- 2.8%; combined 71.7% +/- 1.3% and 83.7% +/- 1.0%. So the results are not a fluke of one random draw (although the data come from the same generator).
  • Alert threshold. The combined view uses risk >= 20. The report shows what other thresholds would do:

8.7 The challenge set (breaking our own system)

The main test is circular: the rules implement the same definitions the generator planted. So challenge.py builds a second ledger with no standard anomalies and plants 20 variants of four kinds:

  • Boundary cases just outside a threshold: a duplicate payment 12 days later (window is 7), a split at 80-88% of the limit (band starts at 90%), a journal of 225,000-245,000 (materiality 250,000), a gap of 30-45 (limit 50), backdating by 20-26 days (limit 30), only two split invoices (minimum is 3), and so on.
  • Disguised cases: an employee's address reformatted ("Bldg" to "Building"), reciprocal approvals (A approves B, B approves A).
  • Controls: cases the rules should catch, to prove nothing is silently broken.
  • Decoys: legitimate entries that look like anomalies, to measure false alarms on look-alikes.

Results (docs/challenge_report.md):

  • Controls: 100% caught by the intended rule (29 entries).
  • Boundary and disguised cases: only 3% caught by the intended rule (175 entries). Thresholds are known to a fraudster and can be avoided.
  • ML rescues one gap: all six journals just under materiality were flagged by ML and reached the queue.
  • Decoys: 100% reached the queue. By data alone, a legitimate large capex accrual looks identical to a suspicious one.

What this proves and does not. It proves where the limits are, with numbers, and that the tests still work. It does not prove real-world performance, because I wrote this set too; the next step is a set written by someone else or real client data.

Bugs found by the tests and the checks, and what they taught (good stories for an interview)

  1. Invoice-number collisions. Injecting several invoices into one gap in a vendor's numbering used up the numbers and reused existing ones, producing accidental duplicates and out-of-order numbers that looked like real findings. Fix: shift later numbers up. Lesson: unexplained false positives often mean your data generator is wrong, not your rule.
  2. Benford contamination. One vendor received three split-purchase groups, so a clean vendor failed Benford. Fix: choose vendors without replacement. Lesson: cross-talk between planted anomalies.
  3. ML entries without a reason. Entries between the 95th and 98th ML percentile earned points but had no flag. Fix: one threshold controls both. Lesson: every queued entry must be explainable.
  4. Silent date swap. Described in 9.3. Lesson: validation must be strict, and a wrong setting must fail loudly.
  5. Sidebar that could not be re-opened. Described in 9.5. Lesson: test with real user actions.
  6. A threshold tuned after seeing results. I tightened the near-duplicate tolerance after a first run showed many coincidences, and the report says so. Lesson: disclose any tuning.

12. Interview preparation: 30 questions with answers

Answer in your own words; the bold phrases are the key ideas.

The project

  1. What is AuditLens in one sentence? A journal-entry analytics tool that combines 23 explainable audit tests with an unsupervised anomaly model into a ranked review queue, evaluated against planted ground-truth anomalies.
  2. Why did you build it? Auditors must look for fraud and error in tens of thousands of entries; analytics tests the whole population and focuses human time on the riskiest entries (ISA 240 expects journal-entry testing).
  3. Why synthetic data? Real ledgers are confidential, and real data has no answer key. Synthetic data lets me plant known anomalies and measure precision and recall. The price is that results show the system works on data like mine, which I state openly.
  4. How is your data realistic? Month-end spikes, seasonal revenue (Ramadan dip), recurring vendors, an approval hierarchy, vendor invoice numbering, the Bahrain Friday/Saturday weekend and public holidays, BHD with three decimals, and Benford-conforming amounts.
  5. What was the hardest part? Making the evaluation honest: preventing label leakage, avoiding generator artefacts that looked like findings, and being candid that the test is partly circular.

Audit and rules

  1. Explain a split purchase. Someone splits one large purchase into several invoices each just under their approval limit, so no higher approval is needed. I flag three or more invoices at 90-100% of a limit from the same creator to the same vendor within 10 days.
  2. Why test weekend postings only for manual entries? Payroll and depreciation run automatically on the last day of the month, even on a weekend. People rarely have a reason to key entries when the office is shut.
  3. What does the post-close test catch? Entries posted after the period closed (month end plus 5 days); they change reported results after management has seen them.
  4. How do you detect a ghost vendor? Match vendor bank account or address to employees after normalising text. I flag every entry of that vendor because the risk attaches to the vendor.
  5. Why a bank-change test? A changed bank account followed quickly by a large payment is the classic business-email-compromise pattern. I flag payments of at least 20,000 and 5 times the vendor's median within 14 days of a change.
  6. Why three duplicate tests? Evidence strength differs: the same invoice paid twice is strongest, an identical amount next, a near amount weakest (coincidences are common), so they carry weights 40, 35 and 8.

Benford

  1. What is Benford's Law? In many natural datasets the leading digit is 1 about 30% of the time and 9 about 5%; P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d). Invented numbers often break it.
  2. How do you test it? Chi-square goodness of fit with 8 degrees of freedom on a vendor's leading digits, flagged at p below 0.001, only with at least 50 invoices.
  3. When does Benford fail? Assigned numbers, capped or contract amounts, narrow ranges, small samples. So a flag is a lead, not proof.
  4. Why such a strict p-value? I test about 55 vendors; at p < 0.001 I expect about 0.05 false alarms, at 0.05 I would expect nearly three.

Machine learning

  1. How does Isolation Forest work? It builds random trees by splitting on random features and values; unusual entries are isolated in fewer splits, so a short average path length means anomalous.
  2. Why not supervised learning? No trustworthy labels in real life, and my planted labels would only teach it my generator.
  3. What are your features? Twenty: amount and account z-scores, vendor frequency and amount statistics, closeness to neighbouring vendor entries, time features, posting lag, manual or automated, user-account and account-pair rarity, and amount relative to the approver's limit.
  4. Why leave the rule signals out of the features? So the ML layer is an independent second opinion; otherwise it would just rediscover the rules.
  5. How do you explain an ML flag? Compare each feature with entries of the same kind (source and vendor group) using a robust z-score (median and MAD), and name the three most unusual features in plain language.
  6. Why is ML precision only 13%? It finds entries that are numerically unusual, but most audit anomalies are relational (a duplicate looks like any payment on its own). It also flags many legitimate large or rare entries. It is a second opinion, and I score it that way.

Scoring and evaluation

  1. Explain the risk score. Rule weights combine by noisy-OR (100 x (1 - product of (1 - w/100)), so more evidence raises the score with diminishing returns and never exceeds 100), plus up to 30 points for ML-flagged entries. It maps to High (50+), Medium (20+) and Low.
  2. Walk through precision and recall. Of what the rules flagged, 84% were real (precision); of what was real, the rules found 100% (recall). F1 balances both. At the top 100 of the queue precision is 100%.
  3. Your recall is 100%. Isn't that suspicious? Yes, and I say so: I wrote the rules and the generator from the same definitions, so it shows correct implementation, not real-world strength. That is why I built an adversarial challenge set.
  4. What does the challenge set show? Controls are caught 100%; boundary cases just outside a threshold only 3%; ML rescues the journals just under materiality; legitimate look-alikes all reach the queue. So thresholds are avoidable and the output is a lead.
  5. What would precision be in a real ledger? With a 0.1% anomaly rate and my 0.32% false-positive rate, about 24%, i.e. roughly three false alarms per real problem. The synthetic 84% is optimistic.
  6. How would you reduce false positives? Feedback loop from reviewers' decisions to re-weight rules, per-client thresholds, fuzzy matching, evidence attachments so legitimate entries can be excluded, and graduated scores near thresholds.

Engineering and deployment

  1. How do you know it works on a fresh machine? GitHub Actions runs ruff and 162 tests on Linux for every push, with pinned dependencies.
  2. How does the upload feature protect against bad data? Column mapping, strict date formats, pydantic row validation with row and column error messages, a balance check on every entry, and warnings about which tests become blind when columns are missing.
  3. How would it scale to millions of lines? The rules are vectorised pandas; the duplicate test does a self-join per vendor, which would need windowed joins or a database at that scale. Move the pipeline to a warehouse or Spark, keep the same logic, and process by period.

Curveballs

  • "What if the fraudster knows your thresholds?" They can stay just under them; the challenge set measures exactly that. Mitigations: randomised or graduated thresholds, vendor-level totals regardless of creator, and the ML layer, which caught the below-materiality journals.
  • "What did you get wrong?" The six stories in Section 11.
  • "Is this production-ready?" No: single synthetic company, no VAT or multi-currency, no audit trail or access control. It is a credible prototype with honest limits.
  • "Does it replace an auditor?" No: it narrows the population and explains why; judgement and evidence remain with the auditor.
